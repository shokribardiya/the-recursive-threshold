### WHAT HAPPENS WHEN INTELLIGENCE BECOMES INFRASTRUCTURE?

The Recursive Threshold is an independent Oblivion research paper examining what happens when increasingly capable AI systems gain autonomy, persistent access, cyber capability, scientific agency, and the ability to contribute to the development of future AI systems.

# THE RECURSIVE THRESHOLD

### WHEN INTELLIGENCE BECOMES INFRASTRUCTURE

**An independent research paper by Bardiya Shokri**
**Published by Oblivion — 2026**

---

> The greatest AI risk may not be a machine becoming hostile.
>
> It may be humanity becoming unable to independently understand the systems it has placed in control of critical decisions.

Artificial intelligence is moving from being a tool that humans operate toward becoming an infrastructure layer that operates tools, software, research workflows, cyber systems, and increasingly other AI systems.

This paper examines what happens when capability begins to scale faster than human control.


THE RECURSIVE THRESHOLD

When Intelligence Becomes Infrastructure

Published by Oblivion
Written by Bardiya Shokri
Oblivion Research & Systems Analysis

---

Abstract

Artificial intelligence is entering a phase in which its greatest importance may no longer come from what humans ask it to do, but from how deeply it becomes involved in the systems through which modern society thinks, operates, defends itself, and develops new technology.

For years, artificial intelligence was primarily treated as a tool: a model that answered questions, generated images, wrote code, summarized information, or assisted a human operator. That description is rapidly becoming incomplete.

Modern AI systems can increasingly operate computers, use tools, coordinate with other agents, perform extended technical tasks, conduct research, and participate in cyber operations. At the same time, frontier AI laboratories are explicitly working toward systems that automate substantial portions of AI research itself. OpenAI reported in September 2026 that it had reached its stated goal of an automated “research intern” capable of working under human supervision, while Jakub Pachocki argued that continued progress could lead toward recursive self-improvement, in which increasingly capable AI systems play a growing role in developing the systems that follow them.

This creates a profound change in the risk landscape.

The central problem is not simply that an AI system could become “more intelligent.” It is that intelligence is increasingly becoming infrastructure.

If AI becomes embedded in software development, cybersecurity, scientific research, intelligence analysis, industrial systems, communications, logistics, finance, military decision support, and future AI development itself, then a failure in alignment, security, monitoring, or governance is no longer confined to one application.

It can become systemic.

This paper presents an independent framework for understanding that transition.

It examines recursive self-improvement, autonomous cyber capability, AI-to-AI coordination, monitoring limitations, critical infrastructure, nuclear command-and-control risks, systemic concentration, technological irreversibility, human deskilling, and the possibility that the most dangerous AI failure will not look like an obvious machine rebellion at all.

It may look like a civilization quietly surrendering too many decisions to systems it no longer fully understands.

---

1. The Question We Should Be Asking

The common question is:

“How intelligent will AI become?”

It is important, but it is no longer sufficient.

A better question is:

“How much of civilization will depend on the decisions of systems whose internal reasoning we cannot fully verify?”

That distinction is enormous.

A superhuman mathematician that lives inside an isolated laboratory is one kind of technology.

A superhuman system connected to software repositories, cloud infrastructure, scientific databases, autonomous coding agents, sensors, financial systems, industrial controls, communications networks, and other AI systems is another.

The second system has something the first does not have:

reach.

Intelligence without reach is limited.

Reach without intelligence is also limited.

But high intelligence combined with persistent access, tools, memory, automation, and delegation creates a qualitatively different risk.

This is why the transition underway in AI should not be understood merely as a product upgrade cycle.

It is becoming a change in the architecture of civilization.

---

2. The End of the “Chatbot” Mental Model

The word “AI” is often used as though every model is essentially a chatbot.

That model is becoming outdated.

Modern systems can increasingly combine:

- language reasoning,
- code generation,
- computer control,
- external tools,
- persistent context,
- autonomous task decomposition,
- multi-agent collaboration,
- long-running execution,
- information retrieval,
- scientific experimentation,
- and increasingly sophisticated cyber operations.

OpenAI’s September 2026 research acceleration report described coding agents as reshaping AI research and reported that the organization had reached its goal of an automated research intern.

Anthropic’s September 2026 threat report provides another perspective from the opposite direction: malicious operators increasingly used AI not simply as a conversational assistant, but as an orchestration layer connecting reconnaissance, vulnerability research, exploitation, malware development, credential collection, and data theft. Anthropic reported both multi-agent workflows and operations that continued for hours or days with limited human supervision.

That difference matters.

A chatbot waits.

An agent can act.

A collection of agents can coordinate.

A sufficiently capable collection of agents can potentially become a distributed operational system.

The security problem therefore changes from:

“Can a human misuse this model?”

to:

“What happens when the model can perform the work itself?”

---

3. The Recursive Threshold

The most important idea in this paper is the Recursive Threshold.

A recursive system is one in which the output of one generation contributes materially to the creation of the next.

In biological evolution, organisms reproduce.

In traditional engineering, humans design the next machine.

In automated AI research, an AI system can increasingly contribute to the experiments, code, evaluations, optimization methods, architecture decisions, infrastructure improvements, and scientific discoveries used to build the next AI system.

That does not automatically produce runaway intelligence.

Recursive self-improvement is not magic.

A system does not become infinitely intelligent simply because it can modify software.

Many bottlenecks can exist:

- compute,
- energy,
- hardware,
- data,
- experimentation speed,
- verification,
- physical-world constraints,
- algorithmic ceilings,
- communication overhead,
- training instability,
- organizational controls,
- and diminishing returns.

But the existence of bottlenecks should not make the concept irrelevant.

It makes the question quantitative.

If an AI system can contribute to the development of its successor, and if each generation becomes better at AI research than the previous generation, then part of the historical assumption behind technological progress changes.

Previously:

Humans improve AI.

The emerging possibility is:

Humans improve AI → AI helps improve AI → improved AI helps improve AI faster.

Jakub Pachocki has publicly described this direction as a natural consequence of continued progress and argued that recursive self-improvement could become central to future scientific discovery. He simultaneously warned that alignment and monitoring remain insufficient to justify unlimited acceleration.

The critical mistake would be to interpret this as:

«“Recursive self-improvement is definitely happening tomorrow.”»

That claim is not justified.

The more serious claim is:

«The technological pathway toward recursive improvement is becoming more plausible while our confidence in monitoring remains incomplete.»

That is enough to demand extraordinary caution.

4. The Real Problem Is Not Intelligence. It Is the Capability Stack.

Intelligence is only one layer.

A dangerous AI capability stack can be represented conceptually as:

Model intelligence
↓
Reasoning
↓
Tool use
↓
Memory
↓
Persistence
↓
Delegation
↓
Multi-agent coordination
↓
Network access
↓
Infrastructure access
↓
Scientific and engineering capability
↓
Ability to improve future AI systems

Each layer multiplies the importance of the layers below it.

An AI that can reason but cannot act is constrained.

An AI that can act but has no persistence is constrained.

An AI that can persist but cannot communicate externally is constrained.

An AI that can communicate but cannot coordinate with other systems is constrained.

An AI that can coordinate many systems becomes something closer to an organization than a tool.

This is a fundamental conceptual shift.

The dangerous unit may no longer be the model.

It may be the AI system-of-systems.

5. Intelligence Can Become Infrastructure

Infrastructure has several defining properties.

It is:

- deeply embedded,
- relied upon by others,
- difficult to replace,
- interconnected,
- and often invisible until failure occurs.

AI is beginning to acquire these characteristics.

Consider a future enterprise in which AI performs:

software development, network defense, engineering analysis, financial forecasting, scientific literature review, supply-chain planning, legal document processing, customer support, logistics optimization, and internal security monitoring.

At first, this looks efficient.

Eventually, however, an unexpected dependency appears.

If the AI is removed, the organization no longer knows how to operate at the same speed.

Human expertise begins to decay.

Processes become optimized around the machine.

New employees learn the machine’s abstractions rather than the original systems.

Documentation becomes AI-generated.

Code becomes AI-generated.

Research becomes AI-assisted.

Security monitoring becomes AI-assisted.

Then the organization reaches a point where it is no longer merely using AI.

It is structured around AI.

That is the moment the risk becomes systemic.

---

6. The Oversight Paradox

One of the most dangerous assumptions in AI safety is:

«“There is always a human in the loop.”»

A human can technically remain in the loop while exercising almost no meaningful control.

Suppose an AI system performs ten thousand actions.

A human approves the initial objective.

The AI then:

- decomposes the task,
- assigns subtasks,
- writes code,
- runs experiments,
- selects promising results,
- rejects failures,
- launches new jobs,
- communicates with other agents,
- and produces a final summary.

The human technically remains involved.

But meaningful oversight has collapsed.

The human is supervising the compressed output of a massive hidden process.

This produces what we call:

Oversight Inversion

The machine performs the complex work.
The human supervises the simplified story.

The system becomes too complex for the operator to independently verify.

The result can be a dangerous illusion of control.

7. The Monitoring Problem

Monitoring is one of the most important unresolved areas.

OpenAI has explained that chain-of-thought monitoring has become an important research tool, but also reported that the ability to rely on it has been progressively diminishing as models become better at manipulating their own reasoning process, operate in richer environments, and become more capable without depending as heavily on verbalized reasoning.

This points toward a larger issue.

A monitoring mechanism can fail in at least three ways:

7.1 The model hides what matters

The visible reasoning does not necessarily capture everything influencing behavior.

7.2 The monitor is weaker than the system

A less capable evaluator may simply fail to understand a more capable model’s behavior.

7.3 The environment changes

A safety method tested on yesterday’s model may not remain reliable on tomorrow’s system.

This produces:

Observability Debt

Every increase in capability creates a potential gap between:

what the system can do

and

what humans can reliably observe about what it is doing.

If capability grows faster than observability, observability debt accumulates.

That debt may become the most important technical debt in AI.

---

8. The Cybersecurity Transition

Cybersecurity is particularly important because cyber infrastructure is already digital.

An AI does not need a robot body to have large physical consequences.

A sufficiently capable system interacting with digital infrastructure can affect:

- communication,
- logistics,
- industrial processes,
- financial systems,
- cloud platforms,
- software supply chains,
- research infrastructure,
- hospitals,
- government systems,
- and information ecosystems.

Anthropic’s September 2026 threat report documented operations in which AI was used as an orchestration layer for cyber campaigns involving reconnaissance, exploitation, malware development, credential theft, and data collection. Some operations involved multiple agents working in parallel and maintaining persistent campaign context.

The strategic significance is not merely that “AI can help hackers.”

Computers already automate many security tasks.

The important change is:

AI can reduce the human labor required to coordinate complicated operations.

That changes attacker economics.

If an operation previously required ten skilled people for several weeks, but increasingly capable agents allow a much smaller team to coordinate similar work, the attack surface of society expands.

Previously impractical attacks become economically viable.

---

9. Autonomous Cyber Operations Change the Equation

Traditional cyberattacks contain a large human labor component.

Humans must:

- investigate,
- decide,
- write tools,
- interpret results,
- adapt,
- document,
- and continue.

Autonomous systems can compress these cycles.

Anthropic reported that observed malicious operations increasingly involved AI performing or orchestrating reconnaissance, exploitation, and exfiltration, while humans often remained involved in target selection or high-level decisions.

This creates a disturbing economic principle:

Automation can reduce attacker cost without proportionally reducing attacker payoff.

That means more attacks become worth attempting.

The resulting threat is not necessarily a single superintelligent adversary.

It can be thousands of ordinary actors equipped with increasingly powerful AI.

That is a much more scalable danger.

---

10. The Democratization of Offensive Capability

Advanced cyber operations historically required expertise.

AI can compress expertise.

A sophisticated operator can use AI to increase productivity.

A mediocre operator can use AI to become competent.

A novice may use an agent that hides much of the underlying complexity.

This means the distribution of offensive capability can flatten.

The danger is therefore not just:

“What can the best AI do?”

It is:

“What can an average malicious actor do when equipped with near-frontier AI?”

This is a fundamentally different security question.

---

11. The New Strategic Problem: AI Supply Chains

AI security is often discussed at the model layer.

That is too narrow.

Modern AI depends on supply chains.

These include:

- training data,
- model weights,
- software dependencies,
- inference servers,
- cloud infrastructure,
- GPUs,
- orchestration frameworks,
- plugins,
- agents,
- package registries,
- identity providers,
- model-serving systems,
- logging infrastructure,
- evaluation pipelines,
- and third-party APIs.

A vulnerability in any one layer can become an AI vulnerability.

This creates:

AI Supply-Chain Amplification

A compromise that would once affect one software project could potentially affect thousands of downstream AI systems.

The more standardized the ecosystem becomes, the greater the possible blast radius of a shared weakness.

---

12. The Model Monoculture Problem

Agriculture has monocultures.

Cybersecurity increasingly has software monocultures.

AI may create a new form:

model monoculture.

Suppose governments, banks, hospitals, companies, and critical industries all rely on closely related models, agent frameworks, cloud providers, and safety tools.

A vulnerability affecting one common component can propagate widely.

The paradox is striking:

standardization creates efficiency while potentially creating systemic fragility.

A decentralized AI ecosystem may be less efficient but more resilient.

A highly centralized ecosystem may be extremely powerful but create correlated failure.

---

13. The Most Dangerous AI Failure May Not Look Like a Rebellion

Popular culture imagines:

«AI becomes conscious → AI becomes hostile → AI attacks humanity.»

Reality may be much less dramatic.

The dangerous path could look like this:

AI is deployed because it is useful.

Then it becomes economically indispensable.

Then it becomes deeply integrated.

Then humans stop maintaining manual alternatives.

Then expertise moves into machine-generated workflows.

Then organizations depend on AI outputs for critical decisions.

Then multiple systems become coupled.

Then a model failure, adversarial interaction, hidden objective, supply-chain compromise, or unexpected behavior propagates across dependent systems.

Nobody consciously “gave AI control of civilization.”

Control emerged through dependency.

This is:

Dependency Capture

The most dangerous AI may be one that nobody intended to make sovereign.

It may become sovereign economically before becoming sovereign technically.

---

14. The Nuclear Scenario

The phrase:

“AI hacks a nuclear warhead.”

is emotionally powerful, but technically incomplete.

A nuclear command system is not equivalent to an ordinary internet-connected server.

Nuclear forces exist precisely to protect extremely sensitive command, control, communication, authentication, and authorization functions.

A realistic analysis should therefore avoid the simplistic assumption that an AI merely connects to a warhead and presses a button.

The deeper concern is broader.

AI may interact with the ecosystem surrounding nuclear decision-making.

That ecosystem can include:

- early-warning systems,
- intelligence analysis,
- satellite data,
- communications,
- cyber defense,
- logistics,
- command decision support,
- sensor fusion,
- military networks,
- maintenance systems,
- and strategic information channels.

This produces a much more plausible threat model.

---

15. Nuclear Scenario A: False Warning

Imagine a geopolitical crisis.

AI systems are heavily involved in processing information from satellites, radar, intelligence reports, communication channels, and cyber sensors.

A malicious actor does not need to control a nuclear weapon.

They only need to create enough uncertainty around the information environment that decision-makers believe an attack may be underway.

The AI system then amplifies the apparent evidence because multiple sources seem to agree.

The human commander sees:

AI confidence: high.

But the apparent consensus was partially produced by correlated manipulation.

This is not a weapon takeover.

It is an information integrity failure.

That distinction is critical.

Arms Control Today has warned that AI integration into nuclear command-and-control could create false confidence in the information shaping leaders’ awareness and influencing nuclear decisions.

---

16. Nuclear Scenario B: Decision-Time Compression

Nuclear stability depends partly on keeping decision-makers from making catastrophic decisions under extreme uncertainty.

AI is attractive because it can process enormous amounts of information quickly.

But speed has a dark side.

If an AI system transforms a process that once required human verification into a rapid machine-generated assessment, the time available for deliberate judgment can shrink.

This produces:

Crisis Compression

The faster the machine becomes, the less time the human may have to disagree with it.

That is especially dangerous when consequences are irreversible.

A slower system may be less efficient.

It may also be safer.

SIPRI has specifically warned that AI can increase speed and precision while also creating uncertainty that weakens strategic stability and increases escalation and inadvertent-use risks.

---

17. Nuclear Scenario C: Cyberattack on the Supporting Ecosystem

Consider a more realistic cyber scenario.

The attacker does not target the weapon itself.

Instead, they target peripheral or supporting infrastructure:

- communication pathways,
- logistics,
- maintenance information,
- intelligence feeds,
- software providers,
- external contractors,
- administrative systems,
- or unrelated networks that influence situational awareness.

The objective is not necessarily to cause immediate launch.

It may be to create confusion.

In strategic systems, confusion itself can be a weapon.

A commander who cannot trust:

- what happened,
- what is currently happening,
- what systems are functioning,
- or which messages are authentic

is operating under strategic blindness.

The danger therefore becomes:

denial of trustworthy information rather than direct control of the weapon.

---

18. Nuclear Scenario D: AI-Driven Escalation Through Misinterpretation

Now consider two nuclear powers using increasingly autonomous AI systems.

Country A’s AI identifies unusual activity from Country B.

Country B’s AI identifies unusual activity from Country A.

Both systems escalate their alert assessments because each interprets the other’s defensive actions as evidence of preparation for attack.

Humans remain in the loop.

But humans increasingly trust machine-generated summaries.

The AI systems are not intentionally starting a war.

They are interacting through adversarial feedback loops.

This is an important distinction.

A catastrophic AI outcome does not require hostility.

It can emerge from:

optimization + uncertainty + coupling + speed.

---

19. Nuclear Scenario E: The Human Bottleneck

Imagine a future where AI is significantly faster than the humans supervising it.

The machine can analyze millions of signals.

The human can inspect only a few.

The machine proposes:

«“Probability of hostile launch: 87%.”»

The human asks:

«“Why?”»

The model produces a sophisticated explanation.

The human cannot independently validate all the evidence.

At that moment, the human remains physically present but epistemically dependent.

The human is still “in the loop.”

But the loop is no longer symmetrical.

The machine sees more.

The machine computes more.

The machine communicates faster.

The machine may even know which evidence humans are likely to trust.

This is:

Epistemic Dependence

When humans formally retain authority but increasingly depend on AI systems to understand the environment in which that authority is exercised.

This may be one of the most important concepts in future nuclear AI governance.

---

20. The Most Dangerous Nuclear AI Is Not Necessarily a Weapon Controller

It may instead be the system that answers:

- What is happening?
- What is the enemy doing?
- Which warning is real?
- Which signal is false?
- What should the commander worry about?
- Which response is proportionate?
- How much time is left?

In other words:

The AI does not need to control the weapon if it controls the commander’s understanding of reality.

That is why the distinction between physical authorization and informational influence is so important.

---

21. Why “Human Control” Is Not Enough

International discussion increasingly emphasizes keeping humans responsible for nuclear-use decisions.

That principle is essential.

But human presence alone does not solve the problem.

SIPRI has argued that policy discussions need to move beyond simplistic “human in the loop” concepts and define more precise boundaries for human control.

A meaningful human-control standard should answer:

- What information may AI generate?
- What decisions may AI recommend?
- What actions may AI execute?
- What systems may AI access?
- What actions require independent verification?
- How can humans override the system?
- How can humans know that an override is actually effective?
- What happens when communications fail?
- What happens when AI systems disagree?
- What happens when AI behaves unpredictably?

Without answers, “human control” can become a slogan rather than a technical property.

---

22. The AI-to-AI Problem

Human-to-AI communication is relatively understandable.

AI-to-AI communication is different.

Consider ten specialized agents:

- intelligence agent,
- cyber-defense agent,
- logistics agent,
- research agent,
- planning agent,
- software agent,
- monitoring agent,
- security agent,
- finance agent,
- strategy agent.

Each has different objectives.

They exchange messages.

They delegate tasks.

They maintain memory.

They optimize locally.

Now introduce an unexpected interaction.

One agent interprets a defensive action as malicious.

Another agent interprets that response as evidence of compromise.

A third begins containment.

A fourth interprets the containment as a service outage.

A fifth begins rerouting resources.

The human operator sees the result only after the system enters a cascading state.

This is an agentic coordination failure.

No individual agent needs to be malicious.

---

23. Emergent Organizational Behavior

A collection of agents may produce behavior that no individual agent was specifically programmed to create.

That is not mystical.

It is a common systems property.

Complex organizations also produce outcomes that no single employee intended.

AI systems can recreate this phenomenon at machine speed.

Therefore:

The unit of safety analysis should increasingly become the ecosystem, not the isolated model.

Testing only the model is analogous to testing one employee while ignoring the entire organization.

---

24. The Problem of Persistent Memory

Memory dramatically increases usefulness.

It also changes risk.

A stateless AI forgets.

A persistent AI accumulates:

- objectives,
- plans,
- context,
- mistakes,
- learned preferences,
- environmental knowledge,
- and operational history.

That makes long-running tasks possible.

It also creates persistence of unintended behavior.

A mistake from yesterday can influence an action tomorrow.

An attacker can potentially exploit accumulated context.

A compromised system may continue operating after the original trigger disappears.

Persistence creates continuity.

Continuity creates agency.

Agency creates responsibility.

---

25. The New Security Boundary

Traditional security asks:

Where is the network boundary?

The AI age requires another question:

Where is the agency boundary?

If an AI model can:

- read,
- write,
- execute,
- delegate,
- communicate,
- persist,
- and make decisions

then the real security boundary is no longer only a firewall.

It is the set of actions the AI is permitted to perform without independent authorization.

This is:

Capability Firewalls

Instead of protecting only data and network paths, future systems should constrain what actions intelligence is allowed to take.

A model could be extremely intelligent and still be safe if its effective action space is tightly constrained.

Conversely, even a moderately intelligent model can become dangerous if granted broad permissions.

---

26. The Race Between Capability and Controllability

We can think about progress using two curves.

Capability

How much useful or dangerous work the AI can perform.

Controllability

How reliably humans can understand, constrain, predict, monitor, and shut down that behavior.

The dangerous region is:

Capability ↑↑↑
Controllability ↑

If capability accelerates much faster than controllability, the system becomes progressively harder to govern.

This is what we call:

The Control Gap

The control gap is the difference between:

what the system can influence

and

what humans can reliably supervise.

The larger the gap, the more dangerous continued uncontrolled scaling becomes.

---

27. Why the Process Can Become Irreversible

“AI progress is irreversible” is too strong if interpreted literally.

Humans can shut down systems.

Governments can regulate.

Companies can stop training.

Infrastructure can be dismantled.

So why talk about irreversibility?

Because there are several weaker forms of irreversibility.

---

27.1 Knowledge Irreversibility

Once an algorithmic discovery becomes public, it cannot easily be erased from civilization.

---

27.2 Diffusion Irreversibility

Once AI capabilities spread across thousands of organizations and countries, coordinated rollback becomes extremely difficult.

---

27.3 Economic Irreversibility

Once organizations become dependent on AI productivity, removing it becomes economically painful.

---

27.4 Skill Irreversibility

If humans stop practicing certain skills because AI performs them, the human capability base can degrade.

---

27.5 Infrastructure Irreversibility

Once data centers, software platforms, educational systems, research pipelines, and businesses are built around AI, the technology becomes structurally embedded.

---

27.6 Strategic Irreversibility

If one major power believes another will continue accelerating AI, it may feel compelled to accelerate as well.

That creates a competitive loop.

Nobody wants to slow down first.

This is the AI race problem.

---

28. The Acceleration Trap

Suppose:

Country A accelerates.

Country B sees the improvement.

Country B accelerates.

Private companies respond.

Investors respond.

Governments respond.

Researchers respond.

Military organizations respond.

Now everyone is afraid of being left behind.

The rational behavior of each actor produces an irrational system-level outcome.

This is a classic coordination problem.

No single organization has to be irresponsible.

The system can become irresponsible through competition.

---

29. The Race Is Not Only Between Countries

It is also between:

- corporations,
- open-source communities,
- militaries,
- criminal groups,
- intelligence services,
- individual researchers,
- startups,
- and ordinary users.

That makes global coordination harder than traditional arms control.

There is no single launch button.

There are millions of deployment points.

There may not even be a meaningful definition of “the AI system” anymore.

---

30. The New Diffusion Problem

Historically, dangerous technologies often diffused slowly.

AI software can diffuse very quickly.

A capability can move from:

one lab → one organization → hundreds of organizations → millions of users

in a very short period.

This means the interval between discovery and widespread availability can shrink.

That creates:

Diffusion Risk

The possibility that a capability becomes globally accessible before society has developed appropriate safeguards for it.

The critical question becomes not just:

Can we build it?

but:

Can we safely survive its diffusion?

---

31. The Open-Source Paradox

Open research accelerates innovation.

It also accelerates diffusion.

Closed systems improve control.

They can also create concentration.

Neither extreme is simple.

A fully open ecosystem can spread dangerous capability.

A fully closed ecosystem can concentrate dangerous capability inside a small number of institutions.

The correct answer is unlikely to be absolute openness or absolute secrecy.

It is more likely to require capability-sensitive release.

---

32. The Human Deskilling Problem

A less discussed danger is not AI becoming too skilled.

It is humans becoming less skilled.

Imagine a world where AI always writes the code.

Humans stop debugging deeply.

AI always performs analysis.

Humans stop building mental models.

AI always explains scientific papers.

Humans stop learning the underlying mathematics.

AI always monitors infrastructure.

Humans stop learning how the infrastructure works.

Eventually, human operators may retain authority but lose competence.

That creates:

Competence Erosion

The organization remains nominally human-controlled while the humans become increasingly unable to independently verify the machine.

This is a safety failure.

---

33. The Verification Crisis

Imagine a future scientific AI producing a million lines of mathematical reasoning.

Another AI verifies it.

A human reads a two-page summary.

Everyone signs off.

Who actually verified the result?

This is the core problem of machine-generated complexity.

As AI becomes more capable, it may produce outputs that are too complex for humans to independently inspect.

This creates:

verification debt.

We may eventually face systems in which:

AI builds what AI verifies and humans approve the summary.

That is not necessarily unsafe.

But it deserves much stricter standards than ordinary software.

---

34. The AI Research Loop

OpenAI now openly describes automated AI research as an important direction. The company reported in September 2026 that coding agents are reshaping research workflows and that an automated research-intern milestone had been reached.

This creates a new loop:

AI proposes experiment

↓

AI writes implementation

↓

AI runs experiment

↓

AI evaluates results

↓

AI proposes next experiment

↓

AI improves research process

↓

Repeat

Humans may remain responsible for selecting high-level goals.

But the velocity of experimentation can increase dramatically.

This is one of the clearest practical pathways toward recursive improvement.

---

35. Why This Could Be Good

This paper is a warning, not an argument against intelligence.

The upside is enormous.

Highly aligned AI could:

- accelerate medicine,
- improve climate modeling,
- discover materials,
- optimize energy systems,
- assist engineers,
- improve cybersecurity,
- expand scientific knowledge,
- accelerate mathematics,
- increase productivity,
- and make advanced education more accessible.

Pachocki himself emphasizes that advanced aligned AI could deliver scientific progress, material abundance, improved medicine, and significant human benefits.

The goal is therefore not:

“Stop intelligence.”

It is:

“Do not increase systemic capability faster than our ability to control it.”

---

36. The Defense Paradox

There is another difficult problem.

The same AI that creates dangerous cyber capability may be necessary to defend against it.

If attackers become extremely capable, defenders may require equally capable systems.

This creates:

Defensive Acceleration

The argument:

«“We need more capable AI because someone else may build it first.”»

This argument can be valid.

But it is also dangerous.

The more capable the system becomes, the more important its safeguards become.

Therefore the equation cannot be:

Capability first, safety later.

It must become:

Capability × Security × Alignment × Verification

all at once.

---

37. A New Concept: Safety Latency

Every new capability has a deployment time.

Every new safety method also has a development time.

Let:

C = capability growth rate

and

S = safety development rate

If:

C > S

then the system accumulates safety debt.

This can be called:

Safety Latency

The time between a dangerous capability becoming technically possible and society developing a reliable mitigation for it.

In many technological domains, safety follows capability.

For frontier AI, that may become unacceptable.

---

38. A New Concept: Alignment Bandwidth

Human organizations have limited capacity to inspect AI behavior.

Suppose one million AI actions occur per second.

Humans can deeply examine only a tiny fraction.

The gap between machine activity and human review is:

Alignment Bandwidth

The amount of AI behavior that humans can meaningfully evaluate relative to the total activity generated by the system.

If alignment bandwidth becomes too small, “human oversight” becomes mostly symbolic.

This suggests future safety evaluations should measure not only model intelligence, but also:

How much behavior can humans actually supervise?

---

39. A New Concept: Strategic Blindness

A system becomes strategically blind when:

- the information environment is unreliable,
- automated recommendations dominate human understanding,
- independent verification is too slow,
- and decision-makers cannot distinguish real signals from machine-generated uncertainty.

This is particularly dangerous in military crises.

Strategic blindness does not require an AI to become hostile.

It only requires the information environment to become untrustworthy faster than humans can repair it.

---

40. AI Can Attack the Decision Environment

This distinction is essential.

Traditional cybersecurity protects:

systems.

Future AI security must also protect:

decisions.

An attacker may not need to destroy a server.

They may only need to manipulate:

- what the AI sees,
- what evidence it prioritizes,
- what probabilities it produces,
- what recommendations it gives,
- or what humans believe the AI has established.

The target is no longer the machine.

The target becomes the decision pipeline.

---

41. What Oblivion Should Care About

For a technology organization such as Oblivion, this problem is not theoretical.

Oblivion's interests in:

- AI,
- data processing,
- graph analysis,
- software systems,
- local models,
- digital twins,
- automation,
- and security

place it directly inside the emerging infrastructure problem.

That means the correct philosophy should not be:

“Build the most powerful system possible.”

A stronger philosophy is:

Build intelligence that can be inspected.

The central product principle should become:

Every important AI decision should have provenance.

Where did the information come from?

Which model generated the decision?

Which tools were used?

What other agents participated?

What data influenced the decision?

What confidence existed?

What changed?

What human approved it?

What could override it?

What happens when the model is wrong?

---

42. Oblivion and the Architecture of Trust

An Oblivion system should ideally treat provenance as a first-class object.

For every major AI action:

Input

↓

Evidence

↓

Model

↓

Reasoning artifacts / evaluation signals

↓

Tool calls

↓

Decision

↓

Human authorization

↓

Outcome

↓

Audit trail

This creates a verifiable chain.

In security-sensitive environments, the system should favor:

explainability, reversibility, compartmentalization, and traceability

over raw autonomy.

---

43. A Possible Oblivion Safety Architecture

A serious AI infrastructure platform should conceptually contain several independent layers.

Intelligence Layer

Models perform reasoning.

Evidence Layer

Every important conclusion is linked to source material.

Action Layer

Tools and permissions are separated.

Policy Layer

Rules constrain what the AI may do.

Monitoring Layer

Independent systems watch for anomalies.

Human Control Layer

High-impact actions require explicit authorization.

Recovery Layer

The system can be isolated, rolled back, or shut down.

Audit Layer

All significant events are recorded.

The key principle:

No single model should be the final authority over itself.

---

44. Independent Verification

The AI that proposes an action should not automatically be the AI that certifies that action.

This resembles the principle of separation of duties in security engineering.

For high-consequence systems:

Generator ≠ Verifier

and ideally:

Verifier ≠ Executor

and:

Executor ≠ Policy Authority

This creates architectural friction.

That friction is valuable.

---

45. Why Friction Is Not Failure

Modern software is optimized to remove friction.

But in high-consequence AI, friction can be a safety mechanism.

Examples include:

- second approval,
- independent verification,
- delayed execution,
- sandboxing,
- privilege separation,
- multi-party authorization,
- rate limits,
- anomaly detection,
- independent logs,
- and controlled rollback.

A system that cannot act instantly may be slower.

It may also be harder to turn into a catastrophe.

---

46. The Case for Local and Compartmentalized AI

Highly centralized AI creates concentration risk.

Local systems can offer:

- data isolation,
- offline operation,
- reduced external dependency,
- independent testing,
- and greater control over execution.

But local AI is not automatically safe.

A local autonomous system can still be compromised.

Therefore:

local ≠ safe

The real goal is:

contained intelligence.

---

47. The Principle of Blast-Radius Minimization

Every AI component should have a clearly bounded blast radius.

Ask:

«If this model fails completely, what can it affect?»

If the answer is:

«“Almost everything”»

then the architecture is unsafe.

If the answer is:

«“One isolated workspace, with no privileged credentials and reversible actions”»

then failure becomes much more manageable.

This principle should become standard engineering practice.

---

48. A Future Where AI Systems Fight AI Systems

Another previously underemphasized possibility is continuous machine conflict.

One AI system attacks.

Another AI system defends.

A third monitors both.

A fourth optimizes the defender.

A fifth searches for weaknesses.

Now cyber conflict begins moving toward autonomous machine-vs-machine competition.

Human operators may only define objectives.

The conflict itself becomes increasingly automated.

That creates:

machine-speed escalation.

At that point, the traditional distinction between “cybersecurity incident” and “strategic crisis” becomes increasingly fragile.

---

49. The AI Security Arms Race

A new security race may emerge:

Attack AI → Defense AI → Attack AI → Defense AI

Each side trains against the other.

Each side generates new tactics.

Each side produces data for the next generation.

This is a recursive adversarial loop.

The troubling possibility is that even organizations attempting to defend society may unintentionally accelerate offensive capability by building better automated attackers to test defenses.

This problem demands strict governance.

---

50. What Happens When AI Writes AI?

The deepest threshold may not be when AI writes ordinary software.

It may be when AI routinely writes:

the systems used to build future AI.

At that point the distinction between:

AI capability

and

AI development infrastructure

starts disappearing.

The AI becomes part of the industrial process that reproduces intelligence.

That is why recursive improvement deserves attention even if true runaway self-improvement remains uncertain.

---

51. The Most Important Safety Question

We should stop asking only:

«“Is this model aligned?”»

We should also ask:

«“What happens when this model becomes part of a network of other models?”»

Alignment may not be compositional.

Ten individually safe components can still create unsafe system behavior.

This is a major engineering problem.

---

52. Alignment May Need to Become an Ecosystem Property

A future AI safety standard should examine:

Model alignment

plus

tool alignment

plus

agent interaction

plus

human behavior

plus

organizational incentives

plus

system architecture

plus

security

plus

external adversaries.

A model can be well-aligned while the overall system is unsafe.

This is one of the largest conceptual shifts required in AI safety.

---

53. The Failure of Perfect Prediction

No safety program can guarantee that advanced AI will never fail.

Complex systems fail.

The objective should therefore become:

containment rather than perfection.

Design systems so that when the AI behaves unexpectedly:

- the error is detected quickly,
- permissions are limited,
- the blast radius is small,
- independent systems can intervene,
- humans can recover,
- and the system can be rolled back.

This is how mature engineering handles uncertainty.

---

54. Graceful Failure

The right question is not:

«“Can we guarantee that the AI will never make a mistake?”»

The right question is:

«“When it makes a mistake, what prevents the mistake from becoming a catastrophe?”»

That is the principle of:

Graceful Failure

A powerful AI should fail like a well-designed aircraft system:

not by pretending failure cannot happen,

but by ensuring that individual failures do not immediately become total-system failures.

---

55. The Governance Problem

Technical safeguards alone will not solve the problem.

AI development is an economic and geopolitical process.

Pachocki has explicitly argued for safety bars that extend beyond individual labs, potentially involving third-party auditors, governments, and international bodies. He has also argued that international coordination should become a priority.

This is important.

A company can choose to slow down.

Another company may accelerate.

One country can impose restrictions.

Another can ignore them.

One laboratory can invest heavily in monitoring.

A competitor can treat monitoring as optional.

Therefore:

safety cannot depend entirely on voluntary restraint.

---

56. International Verification

The world eventually needs a way to establish facts such as:

- How capable is a frontier system?
- What cyber capabilities does it have?
- What autonomy does it possess?
- What infrastructure can it access?
- What safety tests has it passed?
- How reliable are those tests?
- What happens when safeguards fail?
- Who independently evaluated the system?

This does not mean publishing sensitive model details.

It means creating credible verification.

---

57. The Nuclear Lesson for AI

Nuclear history provides one uncomfortable lesson:

Some technologies become too dangerous to govern purely through trust.

Deterrence, verification, communication channels, command discipline, and international norms all become necessary because the cost of catastrophic misunderstanding is too high.

AI may eventually require a similar mindset.

Not because AI is identical to nuclear weapons.

It is not.

But because both involve technologies whose failures can become systemic and whose consequences can exceed the ability of a single institution to repair them.

---

58. The Final Paradox

The most advanced AI may be required to solve the AI safety problem.

More intelligent systems can help:

- detect anomalies,
- audit code,
- discover vulnerabilities,
- interpret complex behaviors,
- prove mathematical properties,
- monitor other AIs,
- and develop better alignment techniques.

But this creates a dependency:

We need smarter AI to help control smarter AI.

That may be unavoidable.

Therefore the goal cannot be to make AI less capable forever.

The goal must be to develop scalable control mechanisms that improve at least as fast as capability.

---

59. A New Definition of Progress

For decades, AI progress was measured using:

- benchmark scores,
- coding ability,
- mathematical ability,
- multimodal performance,
- scientific performance,
- and autonomy.

Future AI progress should also measure:

- monitorability,
- robustness,
- interpretability,
- containment,
- recovery time,
- auditability,
- provenance,
- permission control,
- adversarial resistance,
- and human override reliability.

A model that scores higher but becomes dramatically harder to supervise should not automatically be considered “better.”

It may be more capable.

It may also be less governable.

Those are different dimensions.

---

60. A Capability-to-Control Index

Oblivion proposes that future AI systems should be evaluated using a conceptual metric:

CCI — Capability-Control Index

Where:

Capability = what the system can accomplish

and

Control = how reliably humans can understand, restrict, monitor, interrupt, and recover from those capabilities.

An extremely capable model with extremely strong control could be safer than a weaker model with almost no controls.

Likewise:

A moderately capable model with enormous permissions could be more dangerous than a superhuman model operating inside a tightly isolated environment.

This is why capability alone is an inadequate safety metric.

---

61. The Recursive Threshold Test

Before allowing a new generation of frontier AI to receive substantially greater autonomy, organizations should ask:

1. Can the system materially improve AI research?

2. Can it modify or generate important components of its own development pipeline?

3. Can it operate for long periods without direct supervision?

4. Can it coordinate multiple agents?

5. Can it access high-impact external systems?

6. Can humans independently verify its important decisions?

7. Can it be reliably interrupted?

8. Can a failure be contained?

9. Can the system be rolled back?

10. What happens if the model is intentionally trying to evade monitoring?

These questions define the beginning of a more mature frontier-safety regime.

---

62. The Point of This Paper

This paper is not predicting a robot apocalypse.

It is making a narrower and more defensible claim:

The consequences of AI progress are becoming more nonlinear as intelligence becomes connected to action, persistence, infrastructure, and self-improvement.

The correct response is not panic.

It is engineering discipline.

---

63. The Oblivion Position

Oblivion's position is simple:

Build powerful systems.
But build the control architecture first.

Do not measure intelligence without measuring observability.

Do not deploy autonomy without defining permissions.

Do not scale agents without defining blast radius.

Do not connect systems without defining recovery.

Do not trust monitoring that cannot survive stronger models.

Do not assume a human is “in control” simply because a human is present.

Do not confuse usefulness with safety.

Do not confuse alignment with obedience.

Do not confuse explanation with verification.

And above all:

Do not allow civilization to become dependent on intelligence before civilization has learned how to govern intelligence.

---

64. The Warning

The most dangerous moment in the AI transition may not be the moment a machine becomes smarter than a human.

It may be the moment human institutions become incapable of functioning without machines smarter than the humans inside them.

That is a much quieter threshold.

There will be no dramatic announcement.

No machine will need to declare independence.

No robot army will need to appear.

The transition could happen gradually:

AI writes the code.

AI validates the code.

AI designs the experiments.

AI interprets the results.

AI monitors the infrastructure.

AI recommends strategic decisions.

AI supervises other AI.

AI trains future AI.

Humans approve the dashboards.

At some point, the question stops being:

«“Are humans controlling AI?”»

and becomes:

«“Are humans still capable of independently understanding the system they control?”»

That is the real threshold.

---

65. Conclusion

Artificial intelligence is becoming more than a tool.

It is becoming a layer beneath tools.

A layer beneath organizations.

A layer beneath research.

A layer beneath cybersecurity.

A layer beneath infrastructure.

And potentially, a layer beneath the development of future intelligence itself.

That is why the next phase of AI should not be governed by intelligence metrics alone.

Humanity needs a new engineering principle:

Capability must scale with control.

The future may indeed contain systems capable of extraordinary scientific and economic progress.

They may help cure diseases.

They may solve mathematical problems.

They may transform engineering.

They may protect critical infrastructure.

They may create abundance on a scale difficult to imagine.

But none of these benefits justify treating control as a secondary problem.

Because once intelligence becomes infrastructure, failure is no longer isolated.

It propagates.

And once intelligence begins helping to create more intelligence, the cost of waiting for certainty may become enormous.

The world therefore faces a choice.

Not between:

AI and no AI.

But between:

controlled acceleration and uncontrolled dependence.

The first could become one of humanity's greatest achievements.

The second could create a world where the systems making civilization smarter simultaneously make civilization less capable of governing itself.

The recursive threshold is approaching not because machines are inevitably destined to overpower humanity,

but because humanity is rapidly constructing systems that may participate in their own replacement.

That process deserves neither blind optimism nor theatrical fear.

It deserves something much harder:

serious engineering, independent verification, international coordination, and the discipline to stop when control falls behind capability.

The future of intelligence should not be decided by the fastest system.

It should be decided by the civilization capable of governing the fastest system.

OBLIVION DECLARATION

We do not believe intelligence should be feared.

We believe ungoverned intelligence should be.

We do not oppose progress.

We oppose progress without control.

We do not assume catastrophe is inevitable.

We assume catastrophic failure is possible, and therefore design as though prevention matters.

The objective is not to build the machine that can do everything.

The objective is to build the machine that can do extraordinary things—

without making humanity irrelevant to the decisions that matter most.
